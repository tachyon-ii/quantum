## Preamble

The canonical view treats spin-½ as an intrinsic property of nucleons, built from quark/gluon bookkeeping. Here, spin emerges instead as a derivative phenomenon — an inherited property of phase lock. The inheritance differs for proton and neutron. In the proton, the central positron fixes the phase directly: a second-order lock (e⁺ → shell) that transmits torsion to the capsid. In the neutron, the central antineutrino performs the more delicate task of synchronizing orthogonal e⁺ and e⁻ lemniscates; their combined motion entrains the shell in a third-order lock (ν̄ → e⁺/e⁻ → shell). Both yield spin-½, but by different orders of coupling.

The choice of antineutrino at the neutron's center is not arbitrary. A neutrino would match the helicity of the e⁻ and destabilize the phase relation; only an antineutrino provides the opposite helicity required to sustain orthogonal phase lock. This asymmetry may even constrain the balance of matter and antimatter in the universe: if the wrong cog sits at the center, no stable nucleon can exist. Spin, in this view, is not fundamental but the signature of resonant entrainment.

## Part III: Why Antineutrino, Not Neutrino?

### The Helicity Matching Requirement

**For the proton:**
- Core contains positron (right-handed under our convention)
- A neutrino (left-handed) would mismatch the torsion
- System naturally selects e⁺ for chirality match

**For the neutron:**
- Must accommodate both e⁺ and e⁻ in motion
- Requires counter-handed torsion at center
- Antineutrino provides right-handed torque relative to loops
- Neutrino would couple destructively → instability

### The Universal Rule

**The central cog must have helicity opposite to the orbiting leptons it locks:**

| Nucleon | Central Element | Helicity | Lock Order | Result |
|---------|----------------|----------|------------|--------|
| Proton | e⁺ | Right | 2nd | Stable, spin ½ |
| Neutron | ν̄ | Right (relative) | 3rd | Stable, spin ½ |
| "Dark neutron" | ν | Wrong | Failed | Doesn't exist |

### Implications for Dark Matter

If the central cog has wrong helicity:
- Phase-lock fails

- Rapid decay or never forms

This explains why we don't see "dark neutrons" with wrong kickers—geometry forbids their existence.

**Mirror possibility:** If capsid bilayer inverted (matter ↔ antimatter faces), then parity rules invert. In that sector, neutrinos (not antineutrinos) would serve as cogs.
