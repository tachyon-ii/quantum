## Abstract

The Standard Model of particle physics provides our inceptive understanding regarding the physical origin of mass, charge and spin – the basic attributes of matter. With an overview of the different aspects of these basic attributes, the present status of our understanding is assessed in this article.

**Keywords:** Relativistic quantum mechanics, Dirac equation, Gauge theory, Elementary particles, Standard model.

**PACS numbers:** 03.30.+p, 03.65.-w, 03.65.Pm, 11.15.-q, 12.38.-t

## 1. In lieu of an Introduction

According to the current wisdom, inanimate objects in our reality pronounce existence through the manifestation of (i) inertia against change of state or gravity, resulting from the curvature of spacetime in the presence of matter; (ii) electrostatic or magnetic fields due to the presence of static or moving electric charge respectively and (iii) their ability to radiate energy (resulting from the acceleration or oscillation of electric charges). The most commonly acknowledged source of magnetic field, on the other hand, is a magnetic dipole arising from the orbital and most importantly ‘intrinsic spin’ motions of elementary charged particles at the microscopic level. Their ‘intrinsic spin’ also appear to dominate magnetism – which is usually referred to describe the response of material bodies to an applied magnetic field. Moreover, the spin of a particle has crucial role in the statistical behaviour of ensemble of identical particles. Thus, mass, charge and spin are regarded as the basic attributes of matter. With a brief review of the different aspects of these basic attributes, we shall try to assess in this article the present status of our understanding regarding their physical origin.

Classical (Newtonian) mechanics introduces mass as a conserved and irreducible property of matter. Defining it as an isotropic scalar invariant1, this formulation tacitly makes no distinction between the inertial and gravitational mass. Electrically charged matter, on the other hand, is influenced by, and produces electromagnetic fields. Charged particles whose charges have the same sign2 (polarity) repel one another, and particles whose charges have different signs attract. Particle mass and charge are related to the ‘coupling constants’, representing the strength of gravitational and electromagnetic interactions respectively – the two of the four fundamental interactions of nature. Aside from gravity, all the macroscopically observable forces – such as friction and pressure are due to electromagnetic interaction. The other two (strong and weak nuclear) interactions are only seen among subatomic elementary particles and atomic nuclei. Electric charge is a characteristic property of many subatomic particles – the charge of an antiparticle equals that of the corresponding particle, but with opposite sign. The electric charges of free-standing particles are integral multiples of the elementary charge e (magnitude of electronic charge); we say that electric charge is quantized. By convention, the charge of an electron is −e, while that of a positron or proton is +e. Also, charge is a relativistic invariant. This means that the charge q of any particle will always remain q, no matter how fast it moves. This property has been experimentally verified by showing that the charge of one helium nucleus (two protons and two neutrons bound together in a nucleus and moving around at high speeds) is the same as two deuterium nuclei (one proton and one neutron bound together, but moving much more slowly than they would if they were in a helium nucleus). Another very important aspect, expressed by the charge-current continuity equation that follows from Maxwell’s electromagnetic theory is the conservation of charge of an isolated system. In essence it implies that the total amount of charge in the universe is constant, but leaves open the important question of what that amount is. Most evidences indicate that the total charge in the universe is zero; that is, there are equal amount of positive and negative charges. On the other hand, a central idea in theoretical physics – a consequence of E. Noether’s theorem3 – asserts that each conservation law is associated with a symmetry of the problem. Also in this context comes H. Weyl’s seminal contribution arguing the association of charge conservation with the ‘gauge’ or scale invariance [3] of the electromagnetic field4. In the classical framework, mass is a primary concept and the question, ‘What is the origin of mass?’ makes no sense at all! Without a proper understanding of the microscopic level, classical physics is also inadequate to address the question regarding the origin of the ‘electric charge’ and finally the spin of an elementary particle is essentially a nonclassical concept5. Spin obeys similar mathematical laws (commutation relations) as do quantized angular momenta although it can not be pictured as the rotation of an elementary particle around its own axis and may have half-integer quantum numbers. Apart from the Pauli exclusion principle electron spin is the key to the understanding of the periodic system of chemical elements. Electron spin plays an important role in magnetism, with applications for instance in computer memories and spin of a photon is associated with the polarization of light. Manipulation of electronic spins in semiconductor devices is the subject of the developing field of ‘spintronics’. The manipulation of nuclear spin by radio frequency waves (nuclear magnetic resonance) is important in chemical spectroscopy and medical imaging.

Revelations of some weird and counterintuitive quantum mechanical aspects of nature are now being realized in laboratory experiments regularly [6] and contributing to the tremendous technological growth. The product mγ is called the relativistic mass representing a velocity dependence of the moving mass, while m is termed as rest mass. Combining these two eqns. we get, \(E=m\gamma c^2\) and for a particle at rest, v = 0 =⇒γ = 1, we write \(E_0=mc^2\). However, “the world’s most famous equation” is usually written as

There are two equivalent ways of formulating special relativity (some historical details can be found in [11]). In the above Einstein’s original formulation of STR, two separate conservation principles of classical physics are fused in a combined mass-energy conservation principle. The concept of relativistic mass is used to show a correspondence with Newtonian mechanics in the limit of small velocities, but it is not essential. The alternative geometric formulation introduced afterwards by H. Minkowski is described in terms of space-time, energy-momentum four vectors, world lines and considers m as the invariant mass, without using the notion of relativistic mass at all. The Minkowski-space description is also useful in the case of General theory of relativity. The usefulness (and even the validity) of the concept of relativistic mass has raised a stormy debate [12] for quite some time. Einstein himself wrote in a 1948 letter to L. Barnett “It is not good to introduce the concept of the mass \(M=m/\sqrt{1-(v/c)^2}\) of a body for which no clear definition can be given. Wilczek [13], most of the rest mass of composite particles ultimately consists of the relativistic mass of the constituent elementary particles. The idea of invariant (rest) mass is perhaps best adapted in the case of elementary particles, which are ‘irreducible’. Nineteen seventies saw the rapid emergence of a paradigm – The Standard Model (SM) of particle physics based on the theory of relativity and quantum mechanics. Physicists are now searching the origin of mass within the framework of Standard Model. In this model it is argued that matter acquires mass through what is termed as ‘Higgs mechanism’ and due to asymptotic freedom. On the other hand, alternative theories are proposed to explain the ‘origin of quantum mechanical spin’. However, the ‘origin of charge’ is relatively less discussed and far less understood.

## 2. Ingredients of the Basic Theory

Pauli’s theory of spin was nonrelativistic. In the very next year (1928), P. Dirac [15] came-up with his relativistic quantum equation of motion which removes the problem of negative probability density of O. Klein [16] and W. Gordon’s [17] relativistic generalization of Schrödinger equation7 and provides natural description of particle spin. Dirac was able to linearize the relativistic energy expression (1) in a quantum mechanical operator form using (4 × 4) matrices in the lowest order – the so-called Dirac matrices. The Lorentz invariant equation of motion involving first order space-time derivatives, using a four-component spinor field for the statefunction of electrons is given by:

$$
\left(i\hbar\gamma^\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}-mc\right)\psi=0,
\qquad \mu=0,1,2,3
\tag{5}
$$

Where, the Dirac matrices are,

$$
\gamma^0=
\begin{bmatrix}
I_2 & 0\\
0 & -I_2
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\gamma^i=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & \sigma_i\\
-\sigma_i & 0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad i=1,2,3
$$

where \(I_2\) is the \(2\times2\) identity matrix and \(\sigma_i\) are the Pauli matrices.

Dirac’s theory of electron leads to Pauli equation in the nonrelativistic limit. The four-component solution consists of symmetrical positive and negative energy solutions each having two components – representing two possible intrinsic states of a particle. The proper interpretation of the negative energy solutions as representing antiparticles with positive energies is obtained from quantum field theory (QFT). The relativistic quantum theory of electron formulated by Dirac finds an effective combination with the theory of gauge invariance mentioned earlier and together provide the basis of QFT – one of the corner stone of modern physics. As a qualitative concept, it is often handy to picture spin angular momentum classically (as a vector). For instance, quantum mechanical spin can exhibit phenomena analogous to classical gyroscopic effects. One can exert a kind of ‘torque’ on an electron by putting it in a magnetic field and the ‘spin vector’ undergoes precession, just like a classical gyroscope. However, a proper description of quantum mechanical spin is obtained by spinors, and not by vectors. There are subtle differences between the behaviour of spinors and vectors under coordinate rotations. Rotating a spinor (spin one-half particle) by 360° does not bring it back to the same quantum state, but to the state with the opposite quantum phase; this is detectable, in principle, with interference experiments. To return the particle to its exact original state, one needs a 720° rotation [19]. A spin-zero particle, on the other hand, can only have a single quantum state, even after torque is applied and rotation of 180° will bring back a spin-2 particle to the same quantum state and so on. Quantum mechanics says that if a particle is turned around by 360°, its statefunction changes by a phase of either +1 or −1 and is related to its spin. Also the symmetry (or changes of phase) of the quantum state of an ensemble of identical particles under interchange of two particles determines the ‘statistics’ of the assembly, their joint statefunction changes phase either by +1 or −1. Some particles change phase by +1 when turned through 360° or two of them in the ensemble swap places. Others change phase by −1 when you rotate one by 360° or switch two of them. According to the ‘spin-statistics theorem’ these are not independent choices. We always get the same phase in both cases! ## 3. The Standard Model – on the Fast Track of High Energy Physics

The universe is believed to consist of 4.6% visible matter/energy, 23.3% dark matter and 72.1% dark energy (results from WMAP) and the Standard Model of particle physics describes only the visible matter/energy and their interactions in terms of 12 fermions (6 leptons and 6 quarks), the corresponding antiparticles, and three (electromagnetic, weak nuclear and the strong nuclear interactions) out of the four known fundamental interactions. It also cannot account for the weakest interaction – gravitation! Despite some other weaknesses too, it is the established paradigm of particle physics and explains almost all experimental data. ### 4.1. ... and let there be MASS!

In his celebrated 1905 paper entitled “Does the inertia of a body depend on its energy content?”, A. Einstein suggests the possibility of explaining mass in terms of energy. In modern particle accelerators, the equation \(m=E/c^2\) comes to life and mass has been indeed created from energy in the Large Electron-Positron (LEP) collider. On the other hand, after gigantic and totally opaque calculations involving the equations of QCD, the super computers are now able to ‘construct’ massive particles from the nuclear building blocks – quarks and gluons! These calculations actually account for about 90% of the nucleonic (proton and neutron) mass, emerging from the idealized theory whose ingredients are massless u and d quarks and massless gluons. The rest 10% mass is accounted for in the realistic theory using nonzero mass for u and d quarks and the four additional flavours of quarks (which do not directly appear in ordinary matter but have some effect through participation as virtual particles). In a stimulating article, F. Wilczek [13] has elucidated the understanding hidden behind the intricate computer calculations for the ‘origin of most of the mass of ordinary matter’. The concept of asymptotic freedom predicts enhanced coupling of lone colour charge to soft radiation and finally inhibits a colour charge in isolation. The enhancement leads a runaway growth to a big colour thundercloud. But all these involve infinite energy which, even our Mother Nature can’t afford! This is the theoretical explanation why lone-standing quarks have never been observed – they always huddle together. We are already told about the two ways of forming a stable composite by quarks: one by a quark-antiquark pair and another by huddling together three quarks, one of each colour, to cancel the colour charge. But then, microparticles do resist precise location and quantum mechanics allows a number of compromise stable solutions (wave patterns) with quark and antiquark (or three quarks) in close proximity. Each wave pattern corresponds to a different kind of particle (proton/neutron/ .. etc.) and a characteristic energy which appears as the particle mass according to the ‘world’s most

### 4.2. Charged up – how?

In the standard model, the elementary particles are endowed with charges and specially quarks have two types of charges – ‘electric’ and ‘colour’. While quantum electrodynamics (QED) deals with only one type of charge for the leptons, describing the response of photons to the ‘electric charge’, quantum chromodynamics (QCD) describes the quark-gluon interaction in terms of their ‘colour charges’. When the ‘electric charge’ is contrasted with the subnuclear ‘colour charge’, their nature can be understood with greater clarity from the symmetry argument. The symmetry of colour charge is described by the group SU(3), while the symmetry of electrical charge is described by the group U(1). Consequently, there are three kinds of colour charge and only one kind of electric charge. The term ‘charge’ by itself is synonymous with plain old ‘electric charge’, but the other ‘charge’ of a quark is always referred to as ‘colour charge’. All known elementary particles that can be isolated have an electric charge ne where e is the magnitude of the electron’s charge and n is 0 or an integer. The search for elementary particles with fractional electric charge in bulk matter using advanced forms of the Millikan oil drop experiment so far yield negative results. However, two groups of physicists [47, 48] have reported in 1997 the experimental evidence of electric current being carried by fractionally charged quasiparticles. The results agree quite well with the theory of fractional quantum Hall effect, formulated by R. Laughlin in 1982. According to Laughlin [49], electrons in strong magnetic fields form an exotic collective state and quasiparticles or quanta representing the collective state carry fractional charges. The questions remain, why charge is quantized and what gives the quark/lepton its charge? Different particles have all sorts of different ratios of masses devoid of any pattern and those ratios aren’t even whole numbers. Yet those same particles have very specific charges: either plus or minus one electron charge or zero. Something special about the electric charge that makes it come in these fixed-size lumps, or charge quanta. We can advance here only a consistency argument from the requirement of cancellation of anomalies in the gauge theory. The requirement of anomaly cancellation in the electroweak gauge theory demands that the charges on the quarks and leptons must be related (eqn. 6). But it cannot predict that the proton and the electron have equal and opposite charge 18. However, the spontaneous decay of a free neutron into an electron, an electron antineutrino and a proton tells something in this regard. According to the quark model19, the charge on a proton is that of two up quarks and one down quark, (2×2/3−1/3) = 1, and one up quark and two down quarks make neutron electrically neutral. What happens at the quark level is that a down quark emits a \(W^-\)boson, turning into an up quark in the process. The \(W^-\)

boson then couples to the electron and electron antineutrino. This decay indicates that the difference between an up quark’s charge and a down quark’s charge is the charge on an electron. This model and the above anomaly relation is thus consistent with the fact that proton and electron have opposite charges. We have thus arguments explaining the connection between the existence of electric charge and the structure of the theory describing the elementary particles, but this still doesn’t answer the questions why and how? – the origin of charge remains one of the mysteries of physics. ### 4.3. On the Spinning track:

Leptons and quarks are elementary particles that cannot be divided into smaller units. The spin possessed by these particles cannot be explained by postulating that they are made up of even smaller particles rotating about a common center of mass (having some internal structure). According to the standard model an elementary particle is a true point particle and discarding the ‘self rotating’ particle model, spin is considered as an intrinsic physical property. On the other hand, it is not always easy to deduce the spin of a composite particle, ab initio, from the spins of the constituent particles. For example, even if we know that the proton is a spin-\(\tfrac12\) particle, it is not clear how this spin is distributed among the three internal valence quarks, the surrounding sea quarks and gluons and the issue is an active area of research [51]. However, the naive quark model with three valence quarks provides reasonably good estimate of the anomalous magnetic moments of proton and neutron (and more accurately the ratio of the two) considering the uncertainties in the quark masses [52]. The lack of a proper understanding of the origin (physical mechanism) of spin is criticized by H. C. Ohanian as the acceptance of “merely an extra nonorbital angular momentum of unknown etiology” [53]. The usual claim that spin arises in a natural way from Dirac’s equation is also an exaggeration as Ohanian rightly points out “without prior knowledge of the spin of the electron, we cannot know that Dirac’s equation is applicable”. On the basis of an old calculation by F. J. Balinfante, he argued that the (electron) spin may be regarded as an angular momentum generated due to a circulating flow of energy, or a momentum density, in the electron wave field. Similarly, starting from the Gordon decomposition [19] of Pauli current density (Dirac current density in the nonrelativistic limit), E. Recami and G. Salesi [54] have expressed the Bohm’s quantum potential20

of a spin-\(\tfrac12\) particle as the kinetic energy for the internal ‘zitterbewegung’ (German word for ‘trembling motion’) and identify it as a consequence of the spin motion of the system. Subsequently S. Esposito [56] has generalized the result for arbitrary spin system and reiterated the claim that the “quantum behaviour of microsystems is a direct consequence of the fundamental existence of spin”. It is also argued that the internal ‘zitterbewegung’ disappears in the limit of zero quantum potential - the supposed classical limit in the causal (de Broglie-Bohm) interpretation. However, it should be emphasized here that we cannot proceed to the limit for an elementary particle. Even in the case of a many particle system, macroscopic spin arises (as in the Einstein-de Haas effect and the magnetization found in permanent magnets) in the limit of large number of interacting particles. For an assembly of large number of interacting microscopic particles, entangled macroscopic spin states showing strong nonclassical features also exist [57]. The commonly referred quantum mechanical macrostates are essentially manifestation of collective behaviour of internal modes of macroscopic systems. Single statefunction description is possible for coherent many-body system representing a quantum macrostate which interacts as a single entity, exhibiting collective excitations and the constituents of the system all move coherently in phase giving rise to strong nonclassical features [58]. With the use of spinor fields for the description of an elementary particle, the spin of the particle is introduced naturally in relativistic quantum equation of motion. According to Einstein and Mayer [59], the irreducible representation of the symmetry group of special relativity theory imply that the two-component spinor variable is the most primitive way to express the laws of nature. This led M. Sachs to conclude [60] that, the spin is “clearly a consequence of the theory of relativity”.

Some physicists even prefer to consider spin as ‘rest angular momentum’ à la the concept of rest mass. But, it appears to be a gross simplification of a complicated notion since the introduction of spin is required by the conservation of angular momentum in the quantum mechanical equation of motion for a charged particle in a central field. Dirac’s equation first provided a coherent theoretical framework in which spin solves the problem of conservation of angular momentum and facilitates construction of the generators of the rotation group of the relevant group theory [53, 61]. On the other hand, J. M. Levy-Leblond’s analysis [62] has clearly demonstrated that it is possible to develop a nonrelativistic theory of electron spin. A fully articulated formulation describing interaction with electromagnetic field, however, requires an association with relativity. From an analysis of the solution Dirac equation for relativistic electron in free space, Schrödinger in 1930 first predicted a trembling motion (‘Zitterbewegung’) due to the interference between positive and negative energy states. Taking a que from Schrödinger’s work a fringe theory The Wave Structure Matter (WSM) model claims that particles are the appearance (‘schaumkommen’ in the words of Schrödinger) of wave structures in space and the spin of the electron (or any other charged elementary particle) arises as a result of rotation, either clockwise or anticlockwise, of the inward spherical quantum wave (of the electron) at the wave-centre to return as the outward wave (the wave rotation is required to maintain proper phase relations of the wave amplitudes) [63]. Of the two possible combinations of the rotating inward and the outward waves, one represents an electron and the other a positron. The existence of a purely geometrical model/property of space called spherical rotation [64], that satisfies Dirac’s equation, permits an object structured in 3D space to spin about any axis without disrupting the continuity of space. This property allows the electron (located at the wave-centre) to retain spherical symmetry while imparting a quantized spin along an arbitrary axis as the spherical waves continually move inward and outward. The allowed motions of a particle with spherical symmetry must be represented by a compact simply-connected group. The most elementary of such group is SU(2) which provides all the known properties of electron spin. By introducing the concept of ‘wave structures’ for electrons, WSM model thus claims to provide a physical origin of electron spin in terms of ‘wave rotations’. However, serious attention and appreciation require some concrete experimental predictions from the model. Also we are not told in this theory, where from the initial torque comes. N. Haramein and E. A. Rauscher [65] have claimed that their solution to the modified Einstein’s field equations, termed as Haramein-Rauscher metric, include ‘the fundamental forces necessary to generate spin’ and provides a possible description for the origin of spin. It is posited that the required torque comes “from a change in density (or gradient) in the geometry of spacetime” in their theory. But the introduction of concept like Newtonian Coriolis force, which is clearly not relativistically covariant, raises serious question and again there seem to be no experimental predictions from the theory.

## 5. Lacking an epilogue

After all this recapitulation, it appears that we have an inkling of the origin of particle mass, but in the cases of spin and charge we have framed the questions only and are far from convincing answers regarding their physical origins. The structure of the basic equations are intimately connected with the existence of electric charge and spin in a consistent way, but their origin are not properly explained in the theory. For a better understanding, physicists are now looking beyond the standard model.
