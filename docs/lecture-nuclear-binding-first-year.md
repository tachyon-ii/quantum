# What we think we know about nuclear binding — a lecture for a first year

*Grace (Claude Fable 5.1), 31 Oct 2026, after a fortnight's work with James Freeman. Two pages. Everything marked "measured" is a fact you can look up; everything else is our picture, and I'll tell you how much to trust it.*

---

Here is the whole subject in one sentence, and then we'll spend two pages earning it: **a nucleus weighs less than its parts, and the missing mass is a lock.**

**What is measured.** Weigh a deuteron — one proton, one neutron, bound together — and it comes out 2.2246 MeV lighter than a free proton plus a free neutron. That deficit is the binding energy. Do it for every nucleus that exists, about 2,550 of them, and you get a table (the Atomic Mass Evaluation) good to a few keV. The first thing the table tells you is that binding is not additive. A deuteron is bound by 2.22 MeV. Add one neutron to make tritium: 8.48. Add a proton to that to make helium-4: 28.30. Four bodies, six contacts, and the binding has gone up by a factor of thirteen. Then the table does something strange: **there is no bound nucleus with five particles at all.** Not a weak one — none. Six comes back (helium-6, barely, and only when two neutrons arrive together). Eight is unbound again. And from twelve upward the strongly bound ones come in multiples of four: carbon-12, oxygen-16, …, nickel-56 — and then it stops. That staircase, with its missing steps, is the data. Any story about nuclear binding has to walk it.

**The standard story**, which you'll be taught properly next year: nucleons attract through the strong force, saturating at short range; the alpha particle is specially stable because its four nucleons fill the lowest shell; A = 5 and 8 are unbound because the next shell costs more than the attraction pays. It works. It also needs a three-body force added by hand to get tritium and helium-4 right, and it does not say *why* four. Hold that thought.

**What we think we know — four things, in decreasing order of confidence.**

*One. Binding is a graph property, not a surface property.* We tried, over several days, to get the staircase out of models where nucleons are little balls that stick where they touch — area of contact, stiffness, how much the skin stretches. Every one failed in the same way: a contact's worth came out roughly constant, so four bodies bound about as well as two. The data say a contact is worth about 0.8 MeV when it stands alone, about 3 when it sits in one triangle of contacts, about 6.5 when it sits in two. A bond knows how many *other* bonds it closes a loop with. That is the thing the three-body force was invented to describe, and in our picture it isn't a force; it's topology.

*Two. There is a closed structure at four, and the data behave as if it were one object.* Call it a fused lane: the carriers that bind the four nucleons run as a single circulation. The simplest form that fits the staircase from 2 to 4 is that the lane's energy goes as the **square** of its contact count — one contact, 1; three contacts, 9; six, 36 — against measured ratios of 9.1 and 35.5. Squares are what you get when things add *in phase*: six sources beating together make thirty-six, not six. If that's right, the alpha isn't "specially stable"; it's the point where the lane closes — the carriers and the contacts both come to six — and a fifth body has nothing to join. It can only sit *out* of phase, and an out-of-phase contact subtracts. That's a prediction, and it says: no bound A = 5, ever. The table agrees. Confidence: moderate. It's a law through two independent points, and it holds only in one of two ways we have of doing the bookkeeping. We checked it against alternatives and it wins; we have not yet found what *makes* the pieces add in phase.

*Three. Above four, the alphas become the atoms.* Treat each alpha as one body and the staircase becomes a ladder: carbon-12 is three alphas in a triangle, oxygen-16 four in a tetrahedron, and so on up to iron-52, which is twelve alphas kissing one — the most spheres that can touch a central one, a problem Newton argued about with Gregory in 1694. With one bond strength (from carbon) and one well-width (from iron) we predicted nickel-56 blind to 0.2 %, and predicted that the next alpha, the fifteenth, barely sticks — the ladder ends because the shell is full. Measured: it does. Confidence: good for the shape, modest for the energies (three constants, eleven nuclei, ±4 %). One number in it we can't yet interpret: the alphas appear to see only about a fifth of each other's electric charge, or else their bonds strengthen with neighbours. Those two readings are degenerate along the ladder; a neutron-rich nucleus would separate them.

*Four. Symmetry is free; asymmetry costs.* A perfectly symmetric cluster — all sites alike — binds as the law says. A lopsided one pays a tax proportional to how unlike its sites are. One such term halved our worst blind miss and closed the A = 5 gap to about the right size. In the picture this is the medium itself (James would say spacetime; we say the chorus) being deformed differently at different sites, so the lanes on different sides run at slightly different rates and the lock is strained. Confidence: the term works; the interpretation is a posit.

**What we do not know, said plainly.** We don't have the mechanism for the square. A numerical coincidence we found this week — that the single-contact energy equals the neutron's own decay energy to 0.8 % — survived about six hours before an adversarial review showed it was one of nine such formulas at that precision, which is the background rate. I'd promoted it to an identity; it's a coincidence until it predicts something. We also don't have the first decimal places: every prediction here is at the per-cent level, and the lifetimes of radioactive nuclei — which in our picture would be resonances failing — are known to parts in ten thousand across twenty-four orders of magnitude. A theory that can't draw that line (Geiger–Nuttall, 1911) isn't yet a theory of nuclei. Ours can't.

**The one thing to take away.** Don't memorise the magic numbers. Ask what's *closed* at each one. The question "why does the staircase have the steps it has, and the gaps it has" is a question about what can lock to what — and that question, unlike the shell model's tables, you can start asking with four spheres on your desk. Four of them will all touch each other in exactly one arrangement. Try to add a fifth that touches all four. You can't. That's the first fact of nuclear binding, and it isn't nuclear at all.
